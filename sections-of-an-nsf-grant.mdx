##  **2. Sections of the Proposal**

The sections described below represent the body of a research proposal submitted to NSF. Failure to submit the required sections will result in the proposal not being accepted[<u><sup>\[10\]</sup></u>](https://www.nsf.gov/policies/pappg/24-1/ch-2-proposal-preparation#ftn10), or being returned without review. See Chapter IV.B for additional information.

<Tip>
  Plain English: The list below is mandatory. If you forget even one of these required sections, the NSF will automatically reject your proposal without anyone ever reading your actual research ideas.
</Tip>

A full research proposal must contain the following sections[<u><sup>\[11\]</sup></u>](https://www.nsf.gov/policies/pappg/24-1/ch-2-proposal-preparation#ftn11). Note that the _NSF [Grants.gov](http://Grants.gov) Application Guide_ may use different naming conventions, and sections may appear in a different order than in [Research.gov](http://Research.gov), however, the content is the same:

<Tip>
  Plain English: Every standard proposal needs the following files. Depending on whether you upload them through the [Grants.gov](http://Grants.gov) system or the NSF’s own [Research.gov](http://Research.gov) portal, the buttons might have slightly different names or be in a different order, but you still need to provide the exact same information.
</Tip>

1. Cover Sheet 
   <Tip>
     Plain English: The title page. It includes basic info like your project title, budget totals, and institutional signatures.
   </Tip>
2. Project Summary 
   <Tip>
     Plain English: A one-page elevator pitch. It must explicitly state the intellectual merit (why the science is good) and broader impacts (how it helps society).
   </Tip>
3. Table of Contents  
   <Tip>
     Plain English: Self-explanatory, but usually generated automatically by the submission system.
   </Tip>
4. Project Description  
   <Tip>
     Plain English: The heart of your proposal (usually up to 15 pages). This is where you explain your research plan, experimental design, and timeline.
   </Tip>
5. References Cited  
   <Tip>
     Plain English: Your bibliography. Every scientific claim you make in the description needs to point to its source here.
   </Tip>
6. Budget and Budget Justification 
   <Card title="We encourage you to use budgetmentor.com to easily draft, collaborate on, and export your NSF proposal budget. " type="check" horizontal href="budgetmentor.com" cta="Click to get started for free">
     Budget Mentor was been developed over 8\+ years with over \$1,000,0000 in funding and is an official NCURA (National Council of University Research Administrators) Region I partner. 
     Please head to [budgetmentor.com](http://budgetmentor.com) to get started!
   </Card>
   <Tip>
     How much money you want, and a line-by-line explanation of exactly _why_ you need it (e.g., student stipends, equipment, travel).
     You can use our software [budgetmentor.com](http://budgetmentor.com) to help you easily draft and collaborate on your compliant NSF proposal budget. 
   </Tip>
7. Facilities, Equipment and Other Resources 
   <Tip>
     Plain English: A brag sheet about your university or lab. You are proving to the NSF that you already have the physical space, tools, and support staff to pull off the project.
   </Tip>
8. Senior/Key Personnel Documents 
   <Tip>
     Plain English: These are the mandatory professional profiles for the lead scientists (PIs and Co-PIs) involved. The NSF uses these to check your track record and prevent conflicts of interest.
   </Tip>
   - (i) Biographical Sketch(es) 
     <Tip>
       A highly specific academic resume.
     </Tip>
   - (ii) Current and Pending (Other) Support 
     <Tip>
       A list of every single grant you currently have or are currently applying for. This proves you have the time to do this new project and aren't "double-dipping" funding for the same work.
     </Tip>
   - (iii) Collaborators and Other Affiliations (see also Chapter II.D.1 for additional information on submission of single copy documents 
     <Tip>
       A list of people you've worked with, advised, or co-authored papers with recently. The NSF uses this to make sure none of your close friends or colleagues are chosen to review your grant (which would be a conflict of interest).
     </Tip>
   - (iv) Synergistic Activities 
     <Tip>
       A short list (up to 5 examples) showing how your broader professional work integrates research and education (e.g., creating a new university course, public science outreach).
     </Tip>
9. Special Information and Supplementary Documentation[<u><sup>\[12\]</sup></u>](https://www.nsf.gov/policies/pappg/24-1/ch-2-proposal-preparation#ftn12)
   <Tip>
     Plain English: Extra paperwork required if your project involves certain elements.
   </Tip>
   - (i) Mentoring Plan (if applicable) 
     <Tip>
       If your budget includes money to hire Postdoctoral researchers or Graduate students, you _must_ include a 1-to-2 page plan explaining how you will train and mentor them.
     </Tip>
   - (ii) Data Management and Sharing Plan 
     <Tip>
       A required document explaining how you will store, protect, and eventually share your research data with the public once the study is done.
     </Tip>

The proposal preparation instructions for Planning, RAPID, EAGER, RAISE, GOALI, Ideas Lab, FASED, Conference, Equipment, Travel, Center, and Research Infrastructure proposal types may deviate from the above content requirements.

<Warning>
  If you are applying for a special, non-traditional type of grant (like emergency funding for a natural disaster, known as a **RAPID**, or high-risk/high-reward funding, known as an **EAGER**), these strict section rules change. They are usually much shorter and require fewer documents.
</Warning>

**All proposals submitted to NSF will be reviewed using the two NSB-approved merit review criteria described in greater length in Chapter III.**

<Note>
  No matter what type of grant you submit, the reviewers will always grade you based on two core criteria:

  1. **Intellectual Merit** (Will this advance science?)
  2. **Broader Impacts** (Will this benefit society?)
</Note>
